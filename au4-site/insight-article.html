Turning a business problem into a useful product.

A practical field note from our Product Engineering discipline on turning business friction into a first release people actually use.

By AU4 Labs · Product Engineering

Most product work that disappoints was not badly engineered. It was aimed at a problem nobody had described precisely enough to disagree about.

The gap usually appears early. A business frustration — “reporting takes too long”, “onboarding loses people”, “we cannot launch quickly” — is real, but it is not yet a product brief. Turned straight into a backlog, it produces a system that satisfies the loudest interpretation of the sentence.

Write the problem as a sentence you could be wrong about

A useful problem statement names who is affected, what they are doing when the friction appears, and what it currently costs. “Onboarding loses people” becomes “roughly a third of applicants stop at the document upload step, and support spends two hours a day re-sending links.” That version can be tested, and it points at a first release.

Agree the measure before the design

Deciding what success looks like after a build is finished invites the measure to be chosen to fit the result. Agreeing it first also tends to shrink scope, because most teams discover the measure can move with considerably less software than proposed.

  • One primary measure, expressed in business language.
  • A realistic target range, not a single optimistic number.
  • An agreed way to observe it that exists before launch.

Make the smallest thing people can react to

Prototypes are cheaper to argue with than documents. A clickable flow or a thin working slice surfaces the disagreements that were hidden in the wording of a requirement, and it does so while changing direction is still inexpensive.

What a good first release usually has

  • One complete journey, end to end, rather than several partial ones.
  • Only the integrations that journey genuinely needs.
  • Instrumentation for the agreed measure.
  • A clear list of what was deliberately left out.

Plan the second release while building the first

The quality of a product platform shows up in how easy the second change is. Build the first release so that the next team — often your own — can extend it: tests that describe behaviour, documentation of the decisions and their reasons, and boundaries that keep the risky parts contained.

None of this is complicated. It is mostly the discipline of being specific early, when specificity is uncomfortable and cheap, rather than late, when it is obvious and expensive.

The takeaway

Be specific early, when specificity is cheap. Name the problem precisely, agree the measure before the design, and ship the smallest thing people can react to.
